# Graded Modals — Calibrating Certainty

> [!info] What it does / when to reach for it
> Modals sit on a gradient of confidence and commitment: `will` (certain / promise) → `should` / `ought to` (confident expectation, room left) → `would` (conditional commitment) → `may` / `might` / `could` (open possibility). Reach for this whenever I'm about to state an outcome as if it's guaranteed. In technical and stakeholder writing, **committing exactly as much as the evidence supports protects credibility** — over-claiming is what gets quoted back at you later.

> [!example] My sentence — before → after
> **Before (over-committed):** The consolidation will reduce maintenance overhead.
> *(Sounds like a guarantee. If it doesn't pan out, I own a broken promise.)*
>
> **After (calibrated):** The consolidation **should** reduce maintenance overhead — provided the tenants converge on a shared schema.
> *(Confident expectation, with the condition that actually governs it made explicit.)*

> [!tip] What the structure bought me
> Precision about *how sure I am*. I keep the optimism but stop writing a cheque the architecture might not cash. The reader now knows both my expectation **and** the assumption it rests on.

**Variations worth trying** (same claim, different confidence)
- `…would reduce overhead, if we standardised the configs first.` — conditional, hinges on an action not yet taken
- `…could reduce overhead.` — bare possibility, most tentative
- `…is likely to reduce overhead.` — confident but non-modal hedge
- `…ought to reduce overhead, going by the pilot results.` — expectation backed by evidence

> [!warning] Common trap for me
> Defaulting to `will` / `is` for everything because it sounds decisive. Decisive ≠ accurate. The skill is matching the modal to the actual strength of my evidence.
